What Makes a Great Phaser Pedal

Phaser is one of those effects that players either understand deeply or completely underuse. It is not just a wobbly, psychedelic texture from a 1970s record. A great phaser pedal can add subtle movement to clean tones, thicken up a solo, or put a rotary-style churn under a chord that makes it feel alive. Used with restraint, it is one of the most musical modulation effects on a pedalboard. Used with abandon, it sounds like Eddie Van Halen, David Gilmour, or Stevie Wonder's keyboard player, depending on your settings. All of those outcomes are valid.

Phasers work by splitting your signal and running one copy through a series of all-pass filters, then recombining it with the dry signal. Those filters shift the phase relationship between frequencies at different rates, creating the characteristic peaks and notches in the frequency spectrum. The number of stages, which is the number of those all-pass filter sections, determines the character: 4-stage phasers are subtle and smooth, 8-stage phasers get more dramatic and swooping, and 12-stage units push into jet-plane territory. That stage count matters more than most people realize when choosing a pedal.

The market in 2026 is healthier than ever for phaser pedals. You have faithful analog recreations of classic circuits, boutique boards with stereo outputs and CV control, and digital units that model dozens of vintage units in a single box. Budget options have also improved significantly. You no longer have to spend $200 to get a phaser that doesn't sound thin or plasticky. That said, there is still a real difference between the cheap stuff and the great stuff, and this guide is going to tell you exactly where that line is.

We spent time gigging and recording with all five of these pedals across multiple amp and guitar combinations. The rankings reflect real-world use, not spec sheets.

What to Look for

  • Stage count: This is the single most important spec. 4-stage circuits are classic, funky, and subtle. 8-stage designs get more dramatic and vocal. 12-stage units produce that thick, swirling sound that dominates prog and psychedelic contexts. Some pedals offer multiple stage counts, which is genuinely useful rather than just a marketing feature.
  • Analog vs. digital: Analog phasers have an organic warmth that comes from real phase-shift circuitry. Digital phasers can model multiple characters and offer features like tap tempo and MIDI, but the best ones still put effort into making the sweep feel natural rather than mechanically precise. Both have a place, and neither is inherently better. Know which you need before you buy.
  • Feedback or resonance control: This knob feeds the phased signal back into the input, exaggerating the effect and making the peaks and notches more pronounced. High feedback settings are essential for dramatic, over-the-top sweeps. If you want just one phaser sound, you may not care. If you want range, this knob is important.
  • Rate range: The slowest and fastest extremes of the rate control determine what the pedal is actually useful for. Very slow rates produce a gentle, barely-there movement that works beautifully for clean rhythm playing. Very fast rates push into vibrato and ring-mod territory. A pedal that only covers the middle ground is limiting.
  • True bypass vs. buffered bypass: Phaser pedals with high input impedance buffers can actually improve your signal chain, especially if you run long cable runs or a lot of passive pedals. True bypass is not automatically better here. Check how the pedal sounds when engaged versus off, and also check whether the buffer colors your tone when bypassed.
  • Power requirements: Some older analog phaser designs, particularly those based on vintage circuits using obsolete LFO chips, run on 9V but draw more current than expected. Check the milliamp rating before assuming it will play nicely with a standard one-spot supply or a smaller isolated brick.
  • Size and pedalboard real estate: A phaser you leave on all night takes up permanent space. A phaser you use for two songs per set might be worth getting in a smaller enclosure. Think about how you actually use modulation before defaulting to a feature-packed larger unit.

The Rankings

1. MXR M101 Phase 90
Best overall: the standard by which every other phaser gets judged.

The Phase 90 has been on pedalboards since 1974 and it still earns its spot in 2026. This is a 4-stage analog circuit with a single Rate knob. That is it. No feedback control, no depth knob, no tap tempo. What you get instead is one of the most tonally refined phaser sounds ever put into a box. The sweep is smooth, musical, and never harsh. At slow rates it adds a subtle, organic movement to chords. At faster settings it gets into that classic Van Halen block-letter territory without sounding cheap or clinical.

The Script/Block switch on the current version is genuinely useful, not a gimmick. Script mode is warmer and slightly more subtle. Block mode has a little more presence and top end bite. Both are usable depending on your amp and guitar combination. At around $80 street price, nothing at this price point comes close to this level of analog quality. The Phase 90 is the right answer for most players unless you specifically need features it does not have.

Pros

  • Legendary analog tone that sits perfectly in a mix
  • Script/Block switch adds real versatility
  • Dead simple to use live
  • Excellent build quality for the price

Cons

  • No feedback or depth control limits range
  • Only one stage count, no option to go deeper or more dramatic

2. Source Audio Lunar Phaser
Best feature set: a phaser for players who want everything in one pedal.

The Lunar Phaser is the most capable phaser pedal at a non-boutique price point. It covers 4, 8, 10, and 12 stage voicings, includes both analog and digital modes, offers stereo I/O, tap tempo, MIDI, and integrates with the Neuro app for deep editing and preset saving. In practice, those features are not just spec-list filler. The multi-stage options genuinely sound different from each other, and the ability to store presets means you can dial in a subtle rhythm phaser and a huge lead phaser and recall them instantly.

The analog mode sounds excellent, legitimately warm in a way that earlier Source Audio products sometimes missed. The digital modes are clean and precise. Street price hovers around $200, which feels fair given what you are getting. The Lunar does have a learning curve. It rewards players who are willing to spend an hour with the app. If you want a one-knob solution, buy the Phase 90. If you want a single phaser that covers your whole career, the Lunar is worth the investment.

Pros

  • 4, 8, 10, and 12 stage options in one box
  • Stereo output and MIDI control
  • Neuro app preset storage works reliably
  • Analog mode sounds genuinely warm, not digital-thin

Cons

  • Steeper learning curve than simpler alternatives
  • App dependency for deep editing is not ideal for all players
  • Slightly larger footprint than basic phaser pedals

3. Electro-Harmonix Small Stone
Best for that vintage, wet phaser sound: thick, colorful, and unmistakable.

Where the Phase 90 is smooth and subtle, the Small Stone is thick and syrupy. This is a 4-stage phaser with a Color switch that adds feedback to the circuit, and the difference between Color off and Color on is enormous. Color off is gentle and warm. Color on is lush, almost overwhelming, and produces that liquid dripping quality you hear on classic Gilmour solos and countless 1970s records. The sweep feels slower and heavier than the Phase 90 even at equivalent Rate settings, which makes it a genuinely different tonal character, not just a knockoff.

The Small Stone runs around $70 street price, making it one of the best value phasers available. Build quality is solid. The plastic enclosure gets knocked by some players but it has survived decades of touring rigs and it will survive yours. The Color switch is the whole personality of this pedal. If the Color-on sound is the phaser sound in your head, the Small Stone delivers it for less money than almost any comparable option.

Pros

  • Color switch produces a dramatically different, thick feedback sound
  • Excellent price-to-tone ratio
  • Unmistakable vintage character that cuts through a mix
  • Compact and pedalboard-friendly

Cons

  • Rate range could be wider at the slow end
  • Color switch is on or off, no intermediate feedback control
  • Plastic enclosure is not as confidence-inspiring as steel

4. Walrus Audio Lillian
Best boutique option: analog depth with real tonal flexibility.

The Lillian is a 4 and 6-stage analog phaser with controls for Rate, Depth, Feedback, and a D-P-V switch that toggles between dry, phased, and vibrato outputs. That D-P-V switch is the highlight. Vibrato mode removes the dry signal entirely, giving you a pure pitch-modulated sound that most phaser pedals cannot do. Combined with the Feedback knob and adjustable Depth, the Lillian covers terrain that simpler phasers cannot touch, from barely perceptible shimmer to thick, warbling vibrato.

Street price is around $170, which puts it between the budget options and the Lunar. The build quality is excellent, as expected from Walrus. The 4-stage mode is warm and classic. The 6-stage mode gets more complex and dimensional without tipping into excessive drama. This is the right phaser for a player who wants analog tone and more control than a Phase 90 offers, but does not need MIDI or stereo. The Lillian sounds expensive because it is well made.

Pros

  • Vibrato mode is a genuinely useful addition
  • Feedback and Depth controls add real range
  • 4 and 6-stage switching gives two distinct analog characters
  • Excellent build quality and compact enclosure

Cons

  • No tap tempo, which matters if you play to a click live
  • Slightly pricier than the competition for what it offers versus the Lunar

5. Behringer Vintage Phaser VP1
Best budget pick: a serviceable phase sound for players just getting started.

Nobody is going to pretend the VP1 is a premium product. It is made of plastic, the knobs feel cheaper than everything else on this list, and it runs on a 9V battery with no center-negative DC jack on some earlier production runs. But here is the honest truth: it sounds like a phaser. The sweep is recognizable, the Rate control works across a usable range, and the Color switch produces a thicker feedback sound reminiscent of the Small Stone. For a player who wants to try phaser before committing $70 or more, the VP1 serves that purpose.

Street price is typically under $30, which is the whole argument for it. Once you know you like phaser and you know what character you want, move up to something better. But as a first phaser or a backup board throwaway, the VP1 is not embarrassing. Just do not expect it to survive heavy gigging or last more than a few years of consistent use. It is a tool, not an investment.

Pros

  • Under $30 makes it nearly risk-free to try
  • Color switch adds useful tonal variation
  • Small footprint for a budget pedal

Cons

  • Plastic construction is not built for hard gigging
  • Tone is noticeably thinner than analog alternatives
  • Power supply compatibility has been inconsistent across production runs

Buying Guide

How to Choose

  • If you want one phaser forever: Buy the MXR Phase 90. It is the benchmark for a reason, it fits any genre, and the price is honest. You will not regret it in five years.
  • If you need multiple phaser voices in one unit: The Source Audio Lunar Phaser is the answer. Spend the time with the app, save your presets, and you will have every phaser sound you will ever need without swapping pedals.
  • If you want that thick, wet vintage sound: The EHX Small Stone is the one. Engage the Color switch and dial back the Rate and it nails the Gilmour-era phaser sound more convincingly than pedals that cost three times as much.
  • If you want analog tone with more control than a Phase 90: The Walrus Audio Lillian hits the sweet spot. The vibrato mode alone justifies the price step up from budget options, and the build quality means you are buying something you will actually keep.
  • If you are not sure phaser is for you: Grab the Behringer VP1 and spend a month with it. If you find yourself leaving it on all night, upgrade. If it collects dust, you spent $25 to learn something useful about your tone.
  • Watch your power requirements: The Phase 90 and Small Stone are both 9V standard, but check your isolated supply has enough current headroom. The Lunar Phaser requires a regulated 9V supply and will behave erratically on cheap one-spots. Plan accordingly before your first gig.
Final Verdict

Which Should You Buy?

For most players, the MXR Phase 90 is the correct answer. It is proven, affordable, and sounds better than its price suggests. Players who need more control and multiple stage options should step up to the Source Audio Lunar Phaser, which is genuinely the most complete phaser pedal in this price tier. If your reference point is the classic EHX sound specifically, buy the Small Stone and do not overthink it.

The Walrus Audio Lillian earns its spot for players who want boutique-quality analog tone with the added utility of vibrato mode and a Feedback knob. Skip the Behringer unless cost is truly the deciding factor, and treat it as a stepping stone rather than a destination.
